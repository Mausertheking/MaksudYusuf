IT · Cybersecurity

Trust, made of math

What pulled me into security wasn't hacking scenes from movies — it was the realization that trust itself can be engineered. When you message a friend, pay online, or update your phone, you're trusting mathematics no one can cheat. Cryptography is where philosophy becomes engineering: questions about identity, proof, and truth, answered in code.

My favorite example is the cryptographic hash — a fingerprint for information. It's the same mechanism that signs this very page in the footer below. Try it:

Live demo

The avalanche effect

Type anything, then change a single letter — and compare what happens on the two lines below. Both update live, computed entirely in your browser.

1 · Hex encoding — reversible

2 · SHA-256 fingerprint — one-way

The first line is encoding — it hides nothing; run it backward and your text comes out. The second is cryptography — it cannot be run backward, and one changed letter rewrites half of it. Knowing the difference is where security starts.

Beyond the classroom

The goalkeeper

Football is where I first learned everything I now apply to technology. A goalkeeper doesn't get to make mistakes quietly — every error is a goal, visible to everyone. You learn accountability fast. You learn to communicate, because you see the whole field and your defenders don't. And you learn that ninety minutes of concentration matter more than ninety seconds of brilliance.

90′ of concentration
A striker can miss ten chances and be a hero for scoring one. A goalkeeper — like a security engineer — has to be right every time.

That asymmetry is exactly what draws me to cybersecurity. Defenders don't get highlight reels. They get systems that quietly keep working, and people who never find out how close the shot came.

EcoLens — AI waste sorting for everyone

Point your camera at any piece of waste. EcoLens identifies it and tells you exactly how to recycle it — in Azerbaijani and English.

The problem

Recycling fails at the bin, not in the heart

Most people want to recycle. What stops them is a knowledge gap at the worst possible moment: you're standing in front of the bin, holding something, and you simply don't know which category it belongs to. Multiply that hesitation by millions of people, and good intentions turn into landfill.

That's a philosophical observation as much as a practical one — the gap between knowing what's right and being able to act on it. EcoLens is my attempt to close that gap with engineering: make the right choice as easy as taking a photo.

What it does

Photo in, answer out

You photograph an item. EcoLens's AI vision model identifies what it is, then explains how to dispose of it properly — which bin, what to clean or separate first, and why it matters. Everything works in both Azerbaijani and English, because environmental tools should speak the language of the people using them.

It's a progressive web app, so it installs on any phone straight from the browser — no app store needed. Users get accounts, a scan history, and a daily scan allowance.

How I built it

The stack

  • BackendNode.js + Express — one server handles the API and serves the frontend
  • AI visionGoogle Gemini identifies waste items from photos and generates disposal guidance
  • Data & authSupabase — user accounts, scan history, and per-user daily limits
  • FrontendInstallable PWA, bilingual (Azerbaijani / English)
  • HostingDeployed and live on Render

What it taught me

Building for real users changes everything

EcoLens is deployed and used by real people, which taught me lessons no tutorial covers: rate-limiting an AI API so costs stay sane, designing auth flows people don't abandon, and thinking about abuse before it happens — my first genuine taste of the security mindset I want to study. A live app is a goal you defend every day.
